> **By the end of this lesson you will be able to:**
> - Choose the right join for a question, and avoid the join bug that silently doubles revenue
> - Normalize keys so records from two systems actually match
> - Produce a reconciliation report: matched, missing on each side, and field mismatches
> - Decide which system "wins" for each field

## Two systems, two truths

Almost every customer has the same entity in several systems. Cobalt Supply has accounts in its CRM (owned by Sales) and in its billing system (owned by Finance). The two were never properly connected. So:

- Some accounts exist in the CRM but were never billed (or churned and were deleted from billing).
- Some billing customers never made it into the CRM.
- Where both exist, the **plan** and **monthly amount** don't always agree.

Before you build an AI assistant that answers "what does this customer pay us?", you have to know which answer is right. **Reconciliation** is the process of lining up two sources and reporting exactly where they agree and disagree.

## Keys: the thing everything depends on

A join is only as good as its key.

- **Natural keys** come from the business: an email address, a tax ID, an account number printed on invoices.
- **Surrogate keys** are generated by a system: `A-1001`, a database ID.

When two systems share a key, it's usually been typed or exported differently on each side:

| CRM | Billing | Same account? |
|---|---|---|
| `A-1001` | `a1001` | Yes: case and hyphen differ |
| `A-1003` | `A-01003` | Yes: leading zero added by billing |
| `A-1006` | ` A-1006 ` | Yes: whitespace |

So you write a **normalization function for the key** and apply it to both sides before joining. Be conservative: normalize the differences you've actually seen and confirmed, nothing more. Over-eager normalization merges records that aren't the same.

Also check that keys are **unique on each side** before joining. A duplicate key causes the most dangerous join bug of all.

## Join types in one table

| Join | Keeps | Use it to answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Inner** | Only keys in both | "For accounts in both systems, do the amounts agree?" |
| **Left** | All of A, plus matching B | "For every CRM account, what are they billed?" |
| **Full outer** | Everything from both | "Give me the complete reconciliation picture" |
| **Anti** (A not in B) | A rows with no match | "Which CRM accounts are never billed?" |

A reconciliation is essentially a full outer join, split into its pieces: matched, only in A, only in B.

## The fan-out bug

If a key appears twice on one side, a join produces **every combination** of matching rows:

```
CRM (one row)           Billing (two rows for A-1003, a duplicate)
A-1003  Cobalt Rigging  A-1003  $850
                        A-1003  $850

Joined: two rows for A-1003 → revenue counted twice
```

Nothing errors. The totals are just wrong, and they go into an executive's slide. This is why you check uniqueness first and **fail loudly** if it's violated. In Python, raise an error when building the lookup; in pandas, use `validate="one_to_one"` in `merge`.

## Joining with dictionaries

For reconciliation in plain Python, build a dictionary for each side keyed by the normalized key. Then set operations give you the three groups:

```python
crm = {normalize_key(r["account_id"]): r for r in crm_rows}
billing = {normalize_key(r["customer_ref"]): r for r in billing_rows}

matched = crm.keys() & billing.keys()
only_crm = crm.keys() - billing.keys()
only_billing = billing.keys() - crm.keys()
```

For matched keys, compare the fields that should agree, one by one.

## Comparing fields fairly

- **Map field names.** The CRM's `mrr` is billing's `monthly_amount`. Keep an explicit list of field pairs.
- **Normalize before comparing.** `"Pro"` and `"pro "` are the same plan.
- **Use a tolerance for money.** `1200.00` vs `1199.999` is rounding, not a real mismatch. A tolerance of one cent is typical.
- **Don't compare what's expected to differ.** Account names are typed differently all the time; comparing them produces noise that hides real issues.

## The reconciliation report

The output should be something a finance or ops lead can act on directly:

```
Reconciliation: CRM vs Billing (Mar 10)
  10 matched
   2 only in CRM       → never billed? (A-1009, A-1013)
   1 only in billing   → missing from CRM (A-1020)
   3 field mismatches
       A-1004  plan  CRM=pro   Billing=basic
       A-1006  mrr   CRM=2450  Billing=2405
       ...
```

Each line implies an action and an owner: Finance checks unbilled accounts, Sales adds missing accounts to the CRM, and someone decides the correct plan for A-1004.

## Who wins? Field-level ownership

When two systems disagree, don't pick a winner by gut feel. Agree with the customer, **per field**, which system is the source of truth:

| Field | Source of truth | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly amount | Billing | It's what's actually invoiced |
| Plan | Billing | It drives the invoice |
| Account owner (sales rep) | CRM | Sales manages territories there |
| Industry | CRM | Entered by sales during qualification |

Write this table into your engagement documents. It turns hundreds of future arguments into a lookup.

### The same in pandas

```python
merged = crm.merge(billing, left_on="key", right_on="key", how="outer",
                   indicator=True, validate="one_to_one")
only_crm = merged[merged["_merge"] == "left_only"]
only_billing = merged[merged["_merge"] == "right_only"]
both = merged[merged["_merge"] == "both"]
mismatched_mrr = both[(both["mrr"] - both["monthly_amount"]).abs() > 0.01]
```

`indicator=True` adds the `_merge` column that tells you which side each row came from, and `validate="one_to_one"` raises an error on duplicate keys instead of fanning out.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Normalize keys on both sides (case, whitespace, separators, leading zeros), conservatively.
> - Check key uniqueness before joining; duplicates silently fan out and double totals.
> - Reconciliation = matched + only in A + only in B + field mismatches (with a money tolerance).
> - Agree per field which system is the source of truth.
