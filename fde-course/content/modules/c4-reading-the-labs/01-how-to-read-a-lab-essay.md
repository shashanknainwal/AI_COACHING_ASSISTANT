> **By the end of this lesson** you'll be able to read an essay or policy from a frontier lab and turn it into six short notes: the claim, the best case for it, the assumptions it rests on, the strongest objection, what would change your mind, and what it means for the job you want.

## Why bother reading what labs publish

Let's be honest about this first. **No public source says interviewers quiz candidates on lab essays.** We looked. Candidate accounts of Anthropic's loop describe coding, system design, a project deep dive and a values interview. None of them describe a test on an essay. Don't prepare as if one is coming.

So why spend two hours on this module? Two rounds reward it.

| Where it helps | What happens there | Label |
|---|---|---|
| The written application | A "Why Anthropic?"-style answer, reported as 200–400 words. Generic answers are easy to spot. | Reported |
| The values / culture round | A culture interview in the first onsite loop. Module C5 sets out what else is said about it, and how far each claim can be trusted. | Reported |

In both rounds, the weak answer is praise ("I love your mission") and the strong answer is engagement ("Here's the part of your argument I find convincing, here's where I'm unsure, and here's how that shapes the work I want to do"). Reading the labs' own writing carefully is the cheapest way to have something specific to say.

There's a second reason. As an applied engineer or FDE you will explain your employer's positions to customers: why the model refuses something, what the safety policy means for their launch, how the company thinks about risk. You can only explain what you have actually read.

## The six-note framework

Read the piece once straight through without notes. Then write these six notes. Keep each short. If a note runs long, you haven't understood the piece yet.

### 1. The claim, in two sentences

Write the core argument in two sentences, in your own words, without looking. Sentence one is the thesis. Sentence two is the main reason the author gives for it.

This is harder than it sounds. Long essays have many sections, and it's tempting to list them. Don't list. Ask: if the author could keep only one paragraph, which would it be?

Check your summary against the text afterwards. If the author would say "that's not what I argued", start again. A wrong summary poisons every note that follows.

### 2. The steelman

Write the strongest version of the argument, stronger than the author may have stated it. What is the best evidence for it? Which smart, informed people would agree, and why?

The steelman is not flattery. It's a test of whether you understand why a reasonable person holds this view. If your steelman sounds weak, your critique will be a straw man.

### 3. The load-bearing assumptions

Every argument rests on a few beliefs that, if false, would bring it down. List two to four. Good questions to find them:

- What does the author assume about **timelines** (how fast things change)?
- What does the author assume about **incentives** (what companies, governments and people will actually do)?
- What does the author assume about **the technology** (what it can and can't do, and how well we understand it)?
- What would have to be true about the world for the conclusion to follow?

Mark which assumption carries the most weight. That's where your counterargument goes.

### 4. The strongest counterargument

Attack the most load-bearing assumption, not the weakest sentence. A good counterargument:

- names a specific claim and says exactly why it might be wrong,
- offers evidence, an example, or a mechanism, not just a feeling,
- would make the author pause, not roll their eyes.

Look for published critiques too, and read them with the same six notes. A critique you've only heard about second-hand is not one you can cite.

### 5. What would change your mind

Write down the evidence that would move you, in either direction. "If X happened by Y, I'd update toward the author. If Z, I'd update away."

This is the note interviewers at a mission-driven lab are most likely to respect, because it shows you hold views for reasons and can drop them for reasons. It's the opposite of both cheerleading and cynicism.

### 6. What it means for the job you want

Close the loop. If this essay is right, what does it imply for the work of an applied AI engineer, architect or FDE? Which customers matter most, which risks show up in deployments, what would you do differently on day one?

This note is what turns reading into an interview answer.

## A worked example

Here is the framework applied to a short, real source: Anthropic's [announcement of version 3.0 of its Responsible Scaling Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/news/responsible-scaling-policy-v3) (February 2026). Lesson 4 covers the policy itself. Read the announcement first, then compare your notes with these.

| Note | Example |
|---|---|
| Claim | Anthropic is rewriting its safety policy because some of the "if a model can do X, then we add safeguard Y" rules proved hard to apply and some higher safeguards may be beyond any single company. It now separates what it will do alone from what it thinks the whole industry should do, and adds public safety goals and regular risk reports. |
| Steelman | A commitment you can't meet, or can't tell whether you've triggered, is worse than an honest smaller one. Publishing regular risk reports with outside review gives the public more to check than a threshold nobody can measure. |
| Load-bearing assumptions | That public goals plus transparency create real pressure even when they aren't binding. That the ambiguity in capability thresholds is a measurement problem, not a convenient excuse. |
| Counterargument | Moving items from firm commitments to goals that are graded by the company itself weakens the policy's main strength: binding pre-commitment under competitive pressure. Transparency only constrains behaviour if someone with power acts on what is published. |
| What would change my mind | Toward the author: risk reports that disclose uncomfortable findings, and external reviewers who publicly disagree with the company. Away: goals quietly dropped or regraded without explanation. |
| The job | Customers in regulated industries will ask what these reports mean for their deployment. An applied engineer should be able to explain the difference between a commitment and a goal accurately, without overselling either. |

Notice what the example doesn't do. It doesn't call the policy "inspiring" or "a cynical PR move". It states the argument fairly, then names one specific weakness and what evidence would settle it.

## Common mistakes

- **Summarising the headings.** A table of contents is not a summary.
- **Critiquing the tone.** "It's too optimistic" is a mood. Say which prediction you doubt and why.
- **Borrowing a critique you haven't read.** Link it and read it, or leave it out.
- **Agreeing with everything.** You can want to work somewhere and still disagree with part of its argument. Saying so, calmly and specifically, is a strength.
- **Forgetting the job.** If you can't connect your reading to the role, it won't help you in the loop.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Read lab writing to sharpen "why this lab" and to hold your own in the values round, not to pass a quiz.
> - Six notes: claim in two sentences, steelman, load-bearing assumptions, strongest counterargument, what would change your mind, what it means for the job.
> - Attack the assumption that carries the most weight, with evidence or a mechanism.
> - "What would change my mind" is the note that shows intellectual honesty.
> - Specific engagement beats praise. Fairness beats cleverness.
