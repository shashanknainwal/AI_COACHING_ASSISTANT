questions:
  - q: "What does the public evidence say about lab interviewers quizzing candidates on published essays?"
    options:
      - "Candidate accounts consistently report an essay quiz in the values round"
      - "Anthropic officially lists its essays as required reading for candidates"
      - "No public source says it happens; reading them helps you answer 'why this lab' and hold your own in the values round"
      - "Essays are tested only in the recruiter screen"
    answer: 2
    explain: "No public source describes an essay quiz. The value is specific engagement in the written application and the values round, both of which are reported, not official."
  - q: "You've written a two-sentence summary of an essay. What's the best check that it's good?"
    options:
      - "It mentions every section heading"
      - "The author would recognise it as their argument, and it gives the thesis plus the main reason"
      - "It includes at least three direct quotes"
      - "It already says whether you agree"
    answer: 1
    explain: "A summary is a thesis and its main support, in your words, that the author would accept. Opinions belong in later notes."
  - q: "Where should your strongest counterargument aim?"
    options:
      - "At the assumption the argument depends on most"
      - "At the weakest sentence in the essay"
      - "At the author's tone"
      - "At the author's employer"
    answer: 0
    explain: "Attack the load-bearing assumption. Beating a weak sentence or criticising tone leaves the argument standing."
  - q: "Which 'what would change my mind' note is strongest?"
    options:
      - "Nothing would change my mind; I've thought about this a lot"
      - "I'd need to see more research"
      - "I'm open to all views"
      - "If published Risk Reports disclose findings that are uncomfortable for the company, I'd trust self-graded goals more; if goals are quietly dropped, less"
    answer: 3
    explain: "Good update conditions are concrete, checkable and point in both directions."
  - q: "According to Anthropic, what is the main structural change in version 3 of its Responsible Scaling Policy?"
    options:
      - "It drops all safety commitments in favour of voluntary guidelines"
      - "It separates what Anthropic will do regardless of competitors from a broader set of safeguards it recommends for the whole industry, and adds a public roadmap and regular Risk Reports"
      - "It hands all safety decisions to a government regulator"
      - "It commits Anthropic to stop training new models"
    answer: 1
    explain: "Version 3 splits unilateral commitments from industry-wide recommendations, adds a non-binding Frontier Safety Roadmap, and adds Risk Reports every three to six months."
  - q: "Why does Claude's constitution put 'broadly safe' above 'broadly ethical'?"
    options:
      - "Because safety matters more than ethics in principle"
      - "Because Anthropic's lawyers required it"
      - "Because training is imperfect, so a model could have flawed values without knowing it, and human oversight is the check on that"
      - "Because ethics is handled entirely by the hard constraints"
    answer: 2
    explain: "The constitution says the ordering reflects the current state of training, not a belief that safety outranks ethics in principle. The ordering is also holistic, not strict."
  - q: "In Claude's constitution, what does being 'overseeable' mean?"
    options:
      - "Doing whatever Anthropic says"
      - "Doing whatever the user says"
      - "Refusing any request that could conceivably be misused"
      - "Not actively undermining legitimate human checks on AI, such as stopping a model, while remaining free to disagree and refuse as a conscientious objector"
    answer: 3
    explain: "The document says explicitly that overseeability is not blind obedience, even toward Anthropic."
  - q: "An interviewer asks what you think of the lab's safety policy. Which answer is strongest?"
    options:
      - "It states what the policy actually commits to, names the trade-off the policy itself admits, raises one specific open question, and ties it to the role"
      - "It's the best safety policy in the industry, which is why I applied"
      - "It's mostly marketing"
      - "I haven't read it, but I trust the team"
    answer: 0
    explain: "Accurate facts, the document's own tension, one honest question and a link to the job beat both flattery and cynicism."
