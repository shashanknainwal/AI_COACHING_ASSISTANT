> **By the end of this lesson** you'll have a simple framework for questions about mission, safety, pace and money, know how to disagree respectfully with a lab's stated position, and know the three answer styles that sink candidates.

## What mission questions are for

At a mission-driven lab, some questions aren't about your past at all. They ask what you think. One example said to come up at Anthropic is whether you'd prioritize the mission over the company's future share price (**Reported (secondary sources)**; see lesson 01). Expect questions about safety, the pace of AI progress, and what worries you.

The interviewer isn't checking whether your views match the company's. They're checking three things:

1. **Have you actually thought about it?** Or is this the first time?
2. **Can you hold a tension honestly?** Real views on hard questions include doubt.
3. **Would your motivation survive a hard year?** Lower pay than elsewhere, a slower launch, a decision you disagree with.

## A framework, not a script

Scripts break under the first follow-up. A framework lets you answer the question that was actually asked. For any mission question, try four moves:

| Move | What you say | Example (practice) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Your position** | A direct answer in one sentence. | "Yes, I'd take the mission over the share price, with one condition." |
| **2. Why, from your life** | A reason tied to something you did or saw. | "I've worked at a company that shipped a feature it knew was harmful because of a quarterly target, and I watched what it did to the team." |
| **3. The tension** | What makes it hard, honestly. | "I also have a mortgage. If the equity were most of my pay, I'd feel that." |
| **4. What would change your mind** | The limit of your view. | "If 'mission' became a way to avoid accountability for decisions, I'd push back on that." |

Not every answer needs all four. But moves 1 and 3 are the ones candidates skip most, and they're the ones that sound most like a real person.

## Question types and what to bring

| Type | In the style of (practice) | What a strong answer includes |
|---|---|---|
| **Mission versus money** | "Would you still want this job if the company chose to give up revenue for safety reasons?" | A direct answer, your actual financial reality, and why the work matters to you beyond pay. |
| **Safety** | "What worries you about AI?" | One or two specific concerns you can explain, not a list. Something you've read or seen, with the source. |
| **Pace** | "Is the field moving too fast?" | A view with its strongest counterargument. "Both" is fine if you can say which parts and why. |
| **Your role in it** | "How does an applied role connect to safety?" | Concrete: evals, refusing unsafe customer use cases, honest reporting to customers, feeding failures back to the product. |
| **Disagreement with the lab** | "What do you disagree with us about?" | One considered point, stated fairly. See below. |

## Disagreeing respectfully with a lab's position

Saying you disagree with something a lab has published can be a strength, if you do it well. It shows you read the material and think for yourself. Module C4 teaches a full critique method. In the interview, keep it short:

1. **Steelman first.** State the lab's position in a way its authors would accept.
2. **Name the specific point.** "I'm less sure about X," not "I'm skeptical of the whole thing."
3. **Give your reason.** Evidence, experience, or an assumption you think is shaky.
4. **Say what would change your mind.** This is what separates a view from a reflex.
5. **Stay curious.** "How do people here think about that?" is a fine way to end.

Read the primary sources yourself rather than summaries. For Anthropic, that means essays such as Dario Amodei's [*The Urgency of Interpretability*](https://darioamodei.com/post/the-urgency-of-interpretability) and the company's published policies, covered in module C4. Read them to have real views, not to recite them.

## What not to say

Three answer styles fail reliably.

### Flattery

"I agree with everything you stand for." It's not credible, it gives the interviewer nothing to probe, and at a lab that values honesty it reads as a red flag. Replace it with one thing you find convincing and one question you still have.

### Doom

"We're probably all doomed, but I want to help." Strong concern about risk is a legitimate view held by many people at these labs. Fatalism isn't. If you're worried, say what specifically worries you and what kind of work you think helps.

### Dismissal

"The safety stuff is overblown; I just want to build cool products." You can be skeptical of specific risk claims. Waving the whole topic away at a lab that treats it as central tells the interviewer you haven't engaged with why the lab exists.

## Practice prompts

These are original practice prompts written in the style of reported questions. They aren't real interview questions. Answer each out loud in under two minutes using the four moves.

1. If this company decided not to release a product you'd worked on for a year, because of a risk you didn't fully agree with, how would you react?
2. What's one thing about how AI is being deployed today that you think is going wrong?
3. Tell me about a time you chose the harder, more honest option at work when an easier one was available.
4. What's a belief about AI you've changed your mind on in the last two years?
5. If a large customer wanted to use the model for something legal but that made you uncomfortable, what would you do?
6. Why an AI lab, rather than a well-paid job building AI products somewhere else?

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - Mission questions test whether you've thought about the issue, whether you can hold a tension, and whether your motivation would last.
> - Use four moves, not a script: your position, why from your life, the tension, what would change your mind.
> - Disagree by steelmanning first, naming one point, and saying what would change your mind.
> - Avoid flattery, doom and dismissal. Each one tells the interviewer you haven't engaged.
