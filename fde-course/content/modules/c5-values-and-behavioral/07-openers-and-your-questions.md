> **By the end of this lesson** you'll have a two-minute "tell me about yourself" answer, an honest answer to "why are you leaving?", a clear answer to "why this role rather than a software engineering job?", and a short list of questions to ask your interviewers.

## Why openers matter

Almost every recruiter screen and hiring-manager conversation starts the same way, and the first two minutes set the frame for the rest. Here's what's known:

| Fact | Label |
|---|---|
| Anthropic's careers page says interviewers will ask about your experience and what motivates you, and that you'll have time to ask them about Anthropic. | **Official** ([careers page](https://www.anthropic.com/careers), checked 2026-10-10) |
| Anthropic's recruiter screen is about 30 minutes on background, motivation, level and team fit. | Reported |
| OpenAI's forward deployed recruiter screen covers motivation, your views on AI and why you want customer-facing work. | Reported |

None of these answers is hard. They go wrong because people improvise them. Prepare each one, say it out loud until it sounds like you, and then stop polishing: a slightly rough answer that's clearly yours beats a smooth one that sounds memorised. As module C1 covered, write these yourself first; Claude can critique your draft, not write it.

## "Tell me about yourself": the two-minute pitch

The interviewer isn't asking for your life story. They're asking: who are you professionally, what are you good at, and why are you in this conversation? Answer in four beats, about 250–300 spoken words.

| Beat | What it does | Length |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Now** | Your current role and the one thing you're known for, with a number. | 2 sentences |
| **2. How you got here** | Two past steps that lead naturally to this role. Skip everything else. | 3–4 sentences |
| **3. Proof** | One headline story that matches the job, in one or two sentences. The interviewer can ask for more. | 2 sentences |
| **4. Why this, why now** | Why this role at this company, now. Specific, not "AI is exciting". | 2 sentences |

A practice example for a fictional candidate (write your own; don't adapt this one):

> "I'm a backend engineer at a logistics software company, where I lead the team that owns our customer integrations. Most of what I'm known for is getting messy customer data into a shape our systems can trust. Last year I led the rework that cut customer onboarding from six weeks to ten days.
>
> I started in data engineering, then moved to integrations because I liked being close to customers. Over the last 18 months I've been building LLM features into that work, starting with document extraction.
>
> The project I'm proudest of is an extraction pipeline for shipping documents. I built the eval set from 400 real documents, and it caught a failure mode on handwritten forms before launch.
>
> I'm talking to you because I want that combination of customer work and model work to be the whole job, not a side project, and your forward deployed role is exactly that."

Common mistakes:

- **Starting at university.** Start now, go back only as far as it helps.
- **Reading your resume aloud.** They have it. Give them the thread that connects it.
- **No number.** One concrete number makes the whole pitch more credible.
- **No ending.** Finish with why you're here. Otherwise the interviewer has to ask.
- **Going over three minutes.** Time it. Two minutes is long when you're speaking.

## "Why are you leaving?"

The interviewer is checking two things: that you're moving toward something, and that you won't speak badly about the people you work with.

- **Lead with the pull, not the push.** "I want to work on model deployment full-time" beats "my company is slow".
- **Be honest about the push if there is one,** in one neutral sentence. "We've moved away from the customer-facing work I enjoy most" is fine.
- **If you were laid off, say so plainly.** "My role was cut in a reorganisation in March" is a complete answer. Then move to what you want next.
- **Never criticise your manager or team,** even if they deserve it. The interviewer imagines you saying the same about them.

## "Why this role, and not a software engineering job?"

Applied roles at frontier labs mix engineering with customer work. Interviewers want to know you understand the trade and want it.

| Role | What the job asks for, from the postings | What a convincing answer shows |
|---|---|---|
| **Applied AI Engineer** | A customer-facing technical advisor from discovery through deployment; evals, prompting, agents, retrieval | You enjoy helping other engineers succeed, and you've built evals or LLM features yourself |
| **Applied AI Architect / Solutions Architect** | Pre-sales architecture for large enterprises; fitting the model into their stack | You can design across someone else's constraints and explain trade-offs to technical and business people |
| **Forward Deployed Engineer** | Production builds inside customer systems; 25–50% travel in the posting we read | You like shipping in unfamiliar codebases, and you've thought about the travel |

The posting details are **Official** but change often; module C1 lesson 1 has the snapshot and links.

A strong answer names what you'd **give up** as well as what you'd gain: "I'd own less of one codebase for the long term, and I'm fine with that, because the part of my current job I'd keep if I could keep only one is sitting with a customer's engineers and getting something into production." Naming the cost shows you've thought about it. "I want more impact" with nothing else sounds like you'd take any job.

## Questions to ask your interviewers

You'll usually get five minutes at the end. Use them. Good questions show you're thinking about doing the job, and the answers help you decide.

Rules of thumb:

- **Don't ask what the website answers.**
- **Match the question to the person.** Engineers know the work; managers know priorities; recruiters know process, level and compensation. Keep pay and benefits questions for the recruiter.
- **Ask about the work, not about perks.**
- **Prepare five, ask two or three.** Some will already be answered.

Questions in the style of what works (original; adapt them):

| To | Ask |
|---|---|
| A future teammate | "What did your last week look like, roughly, hour by hour?" |
| A future teammate | "What's a recent deployment that went harder than expected, and what made it hard?" |
| The hiring manager | "What would a strong first 90 days look like in this role?" |
| The hiring manager | "How does the team decide a customer's use case isn't a good fit?" |
| Anyone on an applied team | "Who owns the evals for a customer deployment: your team, the customer, or both?" |
| The recruiter | "What are the remaining stages, and how is level decided for this role?" |

Avoid questions that are really statements ("I've read all your essays, don't you think...?") and questions that ask the interviewer to criticise their employer.

## Practise it

Record yourself giving the pitch and the three answers, with a timer. Listen back once. Then open the checklist.

- [ ] Under two and a half minutes.
- [ ] Starts with now, not with university.
- [ ] Contains at least one real number.
- [ ] Has one proof story matched to the role.
- [ ] Ends with why this role, at this company, now.

- [ ] Leads with what you're moving toward.
- [ ] Any negative is one neutral sentence.

- [ ] Shows you know what the role involves (customers, travel, pre-sales or deployment).
- [ ] Names something you'd give up.

- [ ] None answered on the website.
- [ ] Pay and process questions saved for the recruiter.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - Anthropic says officially that interviewers ask about your experience and motivation and leave time for your questions. Prepare all three.
> - The two-minute pitch has four beats: now, how you got here, one proof story, why this role now.
> - "Why leave?" leads with the pull; any push gets one neutral sentence.
> - "Why this role?" shows you know what the job involves and names what you'd give up.
> - Ask two or three prepared questions about the work, matched to the person in front of you.
