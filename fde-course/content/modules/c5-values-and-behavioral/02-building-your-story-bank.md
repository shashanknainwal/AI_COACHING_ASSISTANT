> **By the end of this lesson** you'll be able to tell a STAR story that spends its words in the right places, list the six stories every applied-AI candidate should have ready, and map almost any behavioral question to one of them.

## Why a story bank

Behavioral interviews feel unpredictable because the questions vary. The stories don't need to. Six well-prepared true stories cover most of what you'll be asked, because most questions are the same few questions phrased differently. Prepare the stories, not the answers.

## STAR, done well

STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. Everyone has heard of it. Most people use it badly: two minutes of context, thirty seconds of action, and a result that's a vague "it went well".

Aim for roughly this split:

| Part | Share of your answer | What it contains |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | about 10% | One or two sentences. Who, where, when. Skip the company history. |
| **Task** | about 10% | What was at stake and what was yours to do. |
| **Action** | about 50% | What *you* did, step by step, including what you considered and rejected. |
| **Result** | about 30% | What happened, with a number if there is one, what went wrong, and what you'd change now. |

Four habits separate strong answers from average ones.

1. **Say "I", not "we".** "We migrated the service" hides your role. "I wrote the migration plan and ran the cutover; two teammates handled the client changes" shows it. Credit others by name, then say what was yours.
2. **Use numbers.** "Cut p95 latency from 4.1 s to 1.3 s." "Three customers churned." "Two weeks late." Numbers make a story checkable and memorable.
3. **Include the cost.** A decision that cost nothing isn't a tradeoff. Say what you gave up.
4. **End with what you'd change.** "Looking back, I'd have raised it a week earlier" signals judgment and stops the interviewer having to drag it out of you.

## Six stories to prepare

Pick a real event for each. One event can cover two slots, but aim for at least four different events so you're not telling the same story all day.

| # | Story | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A disagreement on principle.** You disagreed with someone you respected about what was right, not just about what was faster. | Integrity, fairness to the other side, how you escalate. |
| 2 | **A mistake that hurt others.** Your error cost a customer, a teammate or a team something real. | Ownership, honesty, what changed afterwards. |
| 3 | **Slowing down something not ready.** You delayed or stopped a launch (or wish you had). | Judgment on speed versus safety. Central at mission-driven labs. |
| 4 | **Influencing without authority.** You changed a decision you didn't own. | How you persuade: evidence, relationships, persistence. |
| 5 | **An ambiguous project you scoped.** Vague request, no clear owner, you turned it into a plan. | The core applied-AI skill: scoping. |
| 6 | **A customer you disappointed or saved.** A real person outside your team relied on you. | Customer judgment, communication under pressure. Essential for FDE and architect roles. |

For each, write a short note with the date, the people (roles, not names), what you did, a number, and what you'd change. Don't write full scripts. Scripts sound scripted.

## Mapping questions to stories

Here are common question families, phrased in the style of what candidates report. These are practice phrasings, not real interview questions.

| Question family | Example phrasing (practice) | Best story |
|---|---|---|
| Conflict and disagreement | "Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager." | 1, or 4 |
| Values in tension | "Describe someone you respect but disagree with." | 1 |
| Failure and mistakes | "What's a mistake you made that you still think about?" | 2 |
| Speed versus care | "When did you push back on a deadline?" | 3 |
| Saying no | "Tell me about a time you said no to a customer or leader." | 3, or 6 |
| Influence | "How did you get a team to adopt something they resisted?" | 4 |
| Ambiguity | "Tell me about a project with no clear requirements." | 5 |
| Ownership | "What's something you drove end to end?" | 5, or a strong 4 |
| Customer pressure | "Describe a difficult customer situation." | 6 |
| Learning | "What feedback changed how you work?" | 2, or 1 |

If a question doesn't fit any story, say so and pick the closest: "I don't have a perfect example of that. The closest is..." That's more credible than forcing a fit.

## Keeping your stories honest

Interviewers at these labs are trained to probe. Follow-ups like "what did your manager think?", "what would they say you got wrong?" or "what happened a month later?" take apart embellished stories fast.

- **Don't inflate your role.** If you were one of four people, say so. Your part is still interesting if you describe it well.
- **Don't round the result up.** "It mostly worked, but one customer never adopted it" is stronger than "it was a big success".
- **Don't invent a resolution.** Some disagreements never resolve. Say so, and say what you'd do differently.
- **Don't borrow stories.** A colleague's story falls apart on the second follow-up.
- **Keep confidential details out.** Describe customers by industry and size, not by name, unless the work is public.

That fits here: write your story notes yourself, then use Claude to ask you follow-ups. Don't let a model write the stories. They need to be yours, in your words.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - Spend most of each answer on Action and Result. Say "I", use numbers, name the cost, and end with what you'd change.
> - Prepare six true stories: disagreement on principle, a mistake that hurt others, slowing down something not ready, influence without authority, an ambiguous project, and a customer you disappointed or saved.
> - Most behavioral questions map onto one of those six. Prepare stories, not scripts.
> - Honest stories survive follow-ups. Embellished ones don't.
