> **By the end of this lesson** you'll know where to find a lab's published values, how to check that your story bank covers them, and how to use them as a lens without reciting them back at the interviewer.

## What's published, and what isn't

Many companies publish a short list of values or principles. Anthropic does: its [careers page](https://www.anthropic.com/careers) and [company page](https://www.anthropic.com/company) list seven principles (**Official**, checked 2026-10-10).

What's **not** published: whether or how interviewers score you against that list. Anthropic doesn't publish its values rubric, and no public source says interviewers check answers against these seven headings. So treat the list as a lens for preparing, not as a script. Read the page in full yourself: lists change, and the descriptions under each heading matter more than the headings.

## Anthropic's seven principles, in our words

Summaries are ours; read the originals.

| Principle (as titled on the page) | What it describes, in brief | What it might look like in an applied engineer's story |
|---|---|---|
| Act for the global good | Decisions aimed at good long-run outcomes for humanity, including bold ones | You turned down or reshaped work because of who it could harm |
| Hold light and shade | Taking both the large risks and the large benefits of AI seriously | You argued for a launch and for the safeguard it needed, in the same meeting |
| Be good to our users | "Users" defined broadly: customers, policymakers, colleagues and anyone affected | You went beyond the ticket for a customer, or protected people who weren't your customer |
| Ignite a race to the top on safety | Competing to set a higher bar for safety and security | You raised a quality or security bar others then adopted |
| Do the simple thing that works | Empirical, impact over sophistication; the page's own line is "We don't invent a spaceship if all we need is a bicycle." | You shipped the boring solution, measured it, and skipped the clever one |
| Be helpful, honest, and harmless | High trust, low ego, kind and direct communication, repairing relationships | You gave someone hard news directly, or repaired a working relationship |
| Put the mission first | The mission as the shared purpose and final arbiter | You chose the outcome over your own credit, team or plan |

## Map your story bank

Take the six stories from lesson 02 and build a grid: stories down the side, the company's values across the top. For each story, mark the one or two values it shows most clearly. Then look for gaps.

An example of how the mapping often falls out (yours will differ):

| Story from lesson 02 | Values it often shows |
|---|---|
| 1. A disagreement on principle | Be helpful, honest, and harmless; Act for the global good |
| 2. A mistake that hurt others | Be helpful, honest, and harmless (repairing); Be good to our users |
| 3. Slowing down something not ready | Hold light and shade; Put the mission first |
| 4. Influencing without authority | Ignite a race to the top on safety, if it raised a bar |
| 5. An ambiguous project you scoped | Do the simple thing that works |
| 6. A customer you disappointed or saved | Be good to our users |

Three rules keep this honest:

1. **Never bend a story to fit a value.** If nothing you've done shows a principle, that's fine. Don't invent or inflate. Interviewers' follow-ups find stretched stories quickly.
2. **Don't name-drop.** Saying "this shows I do the simple thing that works" sounds like you memorised the careers page. Tell the story; let the interviewer draw the line.
3. **Prepare one value you find hard.** Pick the principle you're least sure you live up to, or one you have a real question about, and think through what you'd say. That's the material for an honest mission conversation (lesson 04).

## Doing this for any company

The same method works anywhere:

1. **Find the published values.** Usually on the careers page, the "about" or company page, or in a published culture document. If you can't find any, use the job posting's language and the company's own writing.
2. **Copy the list and the short description under each heading.** Note the date you read it.
3. **Build the grid** against your story bank, as above.
4. **Fill the biggest gap** with a story you already have but hadn't written down. Most people have more stories than they think.
5. **Write one honest question** about a value you find hard or ambiguous, for the mission or "questions for us" part of the conversation.

If you're interviewing at OpenAI or Perplexity, look for their own published values or principles and repeat the steps. We haven't verified those pages for this course, so don't rely on any list you see quoted second-hand.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - Anthropic's careers page lists seven principles (Official). How interviewers use them isn't published, so treat them as a lens, not a scoring sheet.
> - Map your six stories against the published values and look for gaps; never bend a story to fit.
> - Tell the story and let the interviewer make the connection. Don't recite value names.
> - Prepare one value you find hard; it's good material for an honest mission answer.
> - For any other company, find its own published values page and repeat the grid.
