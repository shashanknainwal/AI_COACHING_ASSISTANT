> **By the end of this lesson** you'll be able to explain how levels are usually set, list the questions to ask about any offer, and tell stock options, RSUs and profit-style units apart, including vesting, cliffs and what "liquidity" means at a private company.

## What's public, and what isn't

| Fact | Label |
|---|---|
| How Anthropic sets levels | No reliable public source (C1 lesson 5). Ask your recruiter. |
| Compensation bands for a specific role and level | Some job postings publish a salary range, often because local pay-transparency laws require it. Read the live posting for your location. Otherwise ask your recruiter. |
| Which type of equity a given lab grants, and its terms | Not covered here. Terms vary by company and change. Ask your recruiter and read your offer documents. |

Everything else in this lesson is **General**: how levels and equity commonly work across the tech industry. There are no figures here on purpose. Numbers go stale fast, and third-party salary sites mix levels, locations and dates. Use them as a rough sense check at most, and treat the recruiter and the written offer as the source.

This isn't financial, legal or tax advice. For a real offer with significant equity, a short session with a tax adviser who knows equity compensation is often worth it.

## How levels are usually set

At most larger tech companies, level is decided by the people who review your loop, not by you or by your years of experience alone (**General**). The signals that usually matter:

1. **Scope of past work.** What you owned, how many people your decisions affected, how ambiguous the problems were. Years are a proxy for this, and a weak one.
2. **Loop performance.** Especially the rounds that show judgement: system design and the project deep dive. A strong deep dive is often what moves a candidate up a level (**General**).
3. **The role's band.** Some postings are opened at one level or a narrow range.

Why it matters: level usually sets your pay band, your scope and expectations, and how soon you're considered for promotion. A higher level at a lower base can be worth more over a few years than the reverse (**General**).

### Questions to ask about level

- "What level is this role considered for, and how is level decided?"
- "What would distinguish a candidate at the next level up in this loop?"
- After an offer: "What level is this offer at, and what was the main signal?"
- "If I think the scope I've had fits the next level, what evidence would you want to see?"

## The parts of a compensation package

| Part | What it is | Ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Base salary** | Fixed annual pay. | "What's the band for this level?" |
| **Bonus** | Annual or sign-on cash. Some companies have no annual bonus. | "Is there an annual bonus? Is it guaranteed, or based on performance?" |
| **Sign-on** | One-off cash, sometimes clawed back if you leave within a year or two. | "Is there a repayment condition?" |
| **Equity** | Ownership, or a claim tied to value. See below. | Everything in the equity section. |
| **Benefits** | Health, retirement matching, leave, learning budget. Varies a lot by country. | "Can I see the benefits summary?" |

## Equity: the three common kinds

| | Stock options | RSUs (restricted stock units) | Profit or unit-based plans |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What you get** | The right to buy shares later at a fixed price (the strike or exercise price). | A promise of shares (or their cash value) as they vest. | Units tied to the company's profits or value, used by some companies with unusual legal structures. |
| **Worth something when** | The share value rises above the strike price. | Shares have value at all. | Defined by the plan document. Read it. |
| **Costs you money to get?** | Yes: you pay the strike price to exercise, and exercising can trigger tax. | No purchase; vesting is usually taxed as income. | Depends on the plan. |
| **Common at** | Earlier-stage private companies. | Public companies and later-stage private companies. | A minority of companies. |

Key terms:

- **Vesting:** you earn equity over time, commonly over about four years (**General**). Leave early and unvested equity is usually forfeited.
- **Cliff:** nothing vests until a first milestone, often one year, then a block vests at once and the rest vests monthly or quarterly (**General**).
- **Refreshers:** additional grants after you join. Ask whether they're common and how they're decided.
- **Exercise window:** for options, how long you have to buy vested options after leaving. Short windows can force a costly decision. Ask.
- **Valuation:** a private company's share price is set by funding rounds and formal valuations, not a market. A paper value isn't money in your account.

## Liquidity at private companies

Private company equity can't usually be sold on a market. You may only be able to turn it into money when:

- the company goes public or is acquired, or
- the company runs a **tender offer**, where you can sell some shares to approved buyers at a set price, on the company's schedule and terms (**General**).

Tender offers are at the company's discretion: how often, who can take part, and how much you can sell are set by them. Don't assume a past tender means a future one. Ask instead:

- "Has the company offered employees a chance to sell shares, and is that expected to continue? Who's eligible?"
- "Are there restrictions on selling or transferring shares?"

Treat private equity as high-variance: possibly worth a lot, possibly worth nothing, and hard to sell either way. Make sure the cash part of an offer covers your life (**General**).

## A checklist for any offer

1. Level, and what it means for scope.
2. Base, bonus (and whether guaranteed), sign-on and any repayment condition.
3. Equity type, number of units or shares, vesting schedule, cliff, refreshers.
4. For options: strike price, exercise window after leaving, latest valuation basis.
5. For private equity: liquidity history and rules on selling.
6. Benefits summary, start date, location and travel expectations in writing.
7. Offer deadline, and whether it can move.

Get every number in writing before you accept. If something said on a call isn't in the offer letter, ask for it to be added (**General**).

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - How labs set levels isn't public. Commonly, level comes from scope and loop performance, especially design and the deep dive, and it sets your band (**General**).
> - A package is base, bonus, sign-on, equity and benefits. Ask about each one; some postings publish salary ranges.
> - Options cost money to exercise; RSUs don't; profit-style units are defined by their plan. Vesting, cliffs, refreshers and exercise windows matter as much as the headline number.
> - Private equity is hard to sell. Tender offers are at the company's discretion; ask, don't assume.
> - No numbers in this lesson on purpose: your recruiter and the written offer are the sources.
